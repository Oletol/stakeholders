Task 4 – Prepare questions for the client

Prepare a short set of questions that will help you understand the client’s expectations, priorities, available resources and project boundaries.

Prepare your questions around these areas

Current situation Expected result Success criteria Available data Resources Constraints Stakeholders Communication
  1. Write 8–12 questions.
  2. Use open questions when you need explanations or examples.
  3. Use specific questions when you need facts, dates, documents or responsibilities.
  4. Avoid questions that suggest the answer.

  • What existing educational materials or solutions are already in use?

  • Who will approve the final result?
  • How would you prefer us to report progress during the practice period?

Conduct Needs Research

Build an evidence based profile of the target audience using literature, existing research and available secondary data.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this component.
Target audience research

Research before direct contact with the audience

This stage develops a provisional profile of the target audience from secondary evidence. The aim is to understand what is already known about comparable learners before designing an empirical study with the real audience.

Possible evidence sources

  • Academic journal articles and systematic reviews
  • Research reports and sector studies
  • Previous institutional surveys
  • Published learner analytics or participation data
  • Research on comparable professional or educational teams
  • Previous course evaluations, if available

Profile dimensions

Background Prior knowledge Motivation Goals Digital competence Learning behaviour Typical difficulties Barriers Assessment experience Language proficiency (CEFR) Target language situations
CEFR as a shared reference: describe language proficiency with the CEFR Companion Volume (Council of Europe, 2020). It groups communicative activities into reception, production, interaction and mediation. Record the likely level for each activity type, not one overall level: a learner may read professional texts at B2 but speak at B1.
Important: statements based on literature describe comparable populations, not your real audience. They remain provisional until checked empirically.

Conduct Needs Limitations

Includes your group profile and all answers in this component.
Documentation and constraints

Limitations as formal boundaries

This stage is based on documents rather than learner preferences. The aim is to identify formal requirements and restrictions that constrain the future educational solution.

Documents may include

  • Federal and institutional regulations
  • Educational and professional standards
  • Language proficiency frameworks (CEFR) and required target levels
  • Programme specifications and curricula
  • Assessment regulations
  • Accessibility requirements
  • Data protection and privacy rules
  • Academic integrity policies
  • Internal organisational procedures
  • LMS, software and information security requirements
  • Documentation related to workload, scheduling or certification
Key distinction: a limitation is not simply a problem. It is a condition the future solution must respect or work within.

1.4

Conduct Needs Analysis

Design an empirical study of the real target audience and prepare a questionnaire that can validate or challenge previous assumptions.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this component.
Empirical target audience analysis

Design the research for your real target audience

Use the findings from market analysis, stakeholder analysis, literature and documentation to formulate research questions for your real target audience.

The questionnaire should investigate

  • Actual tasks and situations learners face
  • Frequency of those tasks
  • Current level of confidence or competence
  • Self-assessed proficiency using CEFR “can do” statements
  • Target communicative situations, genres and language activities
  • Specific difficulties and barriers
  • Current strategies and tools
  • Previous learning experience
  • Access to technology and organisational constraints
  • Priorities from the learners' perspective
Avoid preference only surveys: “Do you like videos?” is less useful than “How often do you need to review complex material independently and what format do you currently use?”

Quality check

Task 14 – Improve weak questions

Each weak item below contains a typical design error. Study the improved version, then find and rewrite at least three weak items in your own draft.

Questionnaire language: if respondents' English is limited, write the questionnaire in their first language. Otherwise answers reflect reading proficiency rather than needs.
Examples – weak and better items
Vague frequency
Weak: Do you often use English at work?
Better: In the past month, how often did you use English for each of these tasks: emails, online meetings, reading reports, presentations? (never / once or twice / weekly / daily)
Double-barrelled
Weak: How confident are you in reading and writing professional emails in English?
Better: Two separate items: “How confident are you when reading professional emails in English?” and “… when writing them?”
Leading
Weak: Don't you agree that speaking practice is the most important part of an English course?
Better: Rank these activities by how often you need them in your work: speaking in meetings, writing emails, reading documents, listening to presentations.
Unanchored self-assessment
Weak: Is your English good?
Better: CEFR “can do” statement: “I can follow the main points of a work meeting in English when people speak clearly.” (not at all / with difficulty / quite well / easily)
Preference only
Weak: Do you like learning English with videos?
Better: When you need to understand a long English presentation or lecture, what do you usually do? (select all that apply)
Professional jargon
Weak: Do you have difficulties with mediation tasks?
Better: How often do you need to summarise an English document in Russian for a colleague or manager?
