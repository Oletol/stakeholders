Working with Subject Matter Experts

How an instructional designer turns an expert’s experience into a course that learners can actually use

1

Know Your Expert

Understand what the expert brings, what is not their job, and which thinking traps to expect.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this stage.
Theory

Who the subject matter expert is

A subject matter expert (SME) is a person with deep, practice-based knowledge of the field your course is about. The SME is your source of content and quality check. The SME is not a co-designer.

What the expert is good at
  • Has spent many years learning and developing to become a true expert.
  • Masterfully commands the subject area.
  • Knows the subtleties and risks of the profession.
  • Has a wealth of examples, cases and real-life stories from the field.
  • Understands what must be done in practice to truly master the subject.
  • May know a wide range of sources and references on the topic.
What is not the expert’s job
  • Does not know how to teach in a digital environment.
  • Does not speak the instructional design language.
  • Distinguishes learners mainly by their level of preparation and may overlook the other needs we study in the CNA: goals, motivation, working conditions, digital practices, barriers.
  • Knows little about digital delivery formats, learning methods and course organisation in a digital environment.
  • Is not supposed to write course texts, create slides or run webinars.
The division of labour. The expert answers the question “what does good performance look like?” The designer answers the question “how will learners get there?” When either side starts doing the other’s job, the project slows down.
Theory

Why experts cannot simply tell you everything

Much of an expert’s knowledge is tacit: it has become automatic through years of practice, so the expert no longer notices it (Polanyi, 1966). Experts can easily state facts and rules, which is explicit knowledge, but they tend to skip the decisions and small steps they perform without thinking.

Research on cognitive task analysis suggests that experts may leave out up to about 70% of the decisions they make when they describe how they do a task (Clark et al., 2008; Feldon & Clark, 2006). This is not carelessness. It is how expertise works.

Consequence for the interview. Do not ask only “How should it be done?”. Ask the expert to walk through real cases, to describe how a weak performer acts, and to remember being a beginner. These techniques in Stage 2 bring tacit knowledge to the surface.

Theory

Typical biases of subject matter experts

Every expert brings predictable thinking traps into a course project. Knowing them in advance turns “a difficult expert” into a manageable situation.

BiasWhat it isWhat it sounds like
Curse of knowledgeOnce you know something, it is hard to imagine not knowing it (Camerer et al., 1989). The expert skips steps and underestimates difficulty.“It’s obvious, you just write the summary.”
Illusion of others’ competenceExperts systematically overestimate what novices already know and how fast they learn (Hinds, 1999).“Everybody at this level knows the basics.”
Underestimation of learnersThe mirror image of the previous bias. The expert believes the subject is too difficult for learners, so explains every detail at length or simplifies until the content becomes inaccurate.“They will never understand this without the full theory first.” “Just give them a list of phrases, the rest is too complicated.”
Focus on exceptionsRare, dramatic cases are the most memorable, so they crowd out typical everyday tasks.“Let me tell you about the candidate who…”
Redundancy effectThe expert wants to add more and more. In cognitive load theory, extra information that learners do not need reduces learning (Chandler & Sweller, 1991).“We must also include the full history of the standard.”
Status quo and resistance to changePeople prefer the current state of affairs even when an alternative is better (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988).“We have always taught it with lectures, and it works.”
Law of the instrumentA method that has worked for years is applied everywhere, even where it does not fit. “If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail” (Kaplan, 1964; Maslow, 1966). Unlike the status quo bias, the expert may welcome a new course but fills it with the same familiar tool.“Translation and a vocabulary test, that is how I prepare all my students.” “Let’s make every module a webinar.”
Authorship effectPeople value what they have created themselves more highly (the IKEA effect; Norton et al., 2012). Editing feels like loss.“You cannot shorten my text, every sentence matters.”
Effort justificationPeople value what cost them a lot of effort (Aronson & Mills, 1959). The expert believes learners must go through the same long and difficult path. Related to the authorship effect, but it concerns the path, not the text.“I learned it the hard way, from the original sources. They should do the same, otherwise nothing will stick.”

Self

Self-Check. Your Own Thinking Traps

You are becoming an expert in instructional design, and the same biases apply to you.

Before you start. These three activities are not graded and there is no ideal score. They show tendencies in particular situations, not stable personal traits. Your first attempt is saved and visible only to you and to the teacher, who sees a summary by group. Answer honestly: choose what you would actually do, not what seems expected.
Self-assessment 1

Situational Judgement in an Instructional Design Project

Twelve situations from a typical design project. All options are professional and could be defended in a meeting. Choose the one closest to what you would really do. At the end you will see which biases your choices reflect and what each option stood for.

Self-assessment 2

Format Selection for Different Learning Goals

Choose one main format for each of five very different goals. Then look at the pattern of your choices. Do you choose the format from the goal, or do you have a favourite tool?

Self-assessment 3

Estimating What Others Know

For each term, answer two questions. Could you explain it to a client in two sentences right now? What share of the students on this course could do the same? Your estimates are then compared with the real answers of the course. This shows how well you judge other people’s knowledge, which is the mechanism behind the curse of knowledge.

Prepare the Interview

Plan a focused conversation that collects actions, mistakes, criteria and cases rather than a lecture on the subject.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this stage.
Theory

Structure of an interview with an expert

  1. Introduction. Purpose of the meeting, how long it takes, how the results will be used.
  2. Tasks. What learners must be able to do in real situations.
  3. Process. How a competent person performs each task, step by step.
  4. Mistakes. Where beginners go wrong, which errors are critical.
  5. Assessment. How the expert tells strong performance from weak.
  6. Examples. Cases, stories, model answers, authentic materials.
  7. Closing. Teach-back summary, next steps, date of the follow-up.
Teach-back. At the end of the interview, retell in two or three sentences what you understood and ask the expert to correct you. It takes two minutes and catches most misunderstandings before they reach the course.
Two experts are better than one. If your client can offer a second expert, ask both the key questions. Where they agree, you have core content. Where they disagree, you have found an exception or a matter of personal style.
Question bank
Sample questions for each purpose

Open the bank when you write your own interview guide in Task 4. Try the exercise below first.

1 Open-ended questions. Starting the conversation

Help the expert warm up and reveal context.

  • What is considered most important in your subject?
  • What types of tasks do you deal with most frequently?
  • What mistakes do beginners often make?
  • What challenges do your students or trainees usually face?
  • In your view, what distinguishes excellent performance from acceptable?
2 Action questions. Identifying what learners must do
  • What exactly should a learner be able to do after the course?
  • How do you know when a student has mastered a particular skill?
  • Could you describe, step by step, how you teach or assess this skill?
  • What should a learner be able to do before starting this stage?
3 Clarifying questions. Making answers specific
  • What does “understanding the model” mean in practical terms? How is it shown in learner behaviour or output?
  • What happens if a learner does this incorrectly?
  • What does a well-performed task look like?
  • Could you give a concrete classroom or exam example?
  • How do you check that a learner has really acquired the skill?
4 Diagnostic questions. Finding where errors occur
  • Where do errors occur most often in this skill area?
  • Which mistakes are critical, and which are minor or acceptable?
5 Case and context questions. Collecting examples
  • Could you describe a typical exam situation?
  • What was the most illustrative case or success story from your experience?
  • What real-life difficulties or objections do learners encounter?
  • What do you usually say when you explain this concept to a student for the first time?
6 Essentials questions. Filtering out the noise
  • Without which knowledge or skill could a learner not perform effectively?
  • What could be provided as optional reference material instead of core training?
  • What needs to be mastered immediately, and what can be developed later through practice?
  • Which elements are imperative and which are merely desirable?
7 Assessment criteria questions. Telling strong from weak
  • How do you tell a strong performance from a weak one?
  • Do you have a checklist or marking criteria for evaluation?
  • What kinds of mistakes are absolutely unacceptable?
Theory

Techniques that bring tacit knowledge to the surface

Real case

Ask about a real-life situation. What happened? How did you act? What went well and what went wrong?

Works especially well when the expert cannot describe “how it should be” but can recount what actually happened. The technique goes back to the critical incident method (Flanagan, 1954).

Employee with an error

Ask the expert to describe how a person who struggles performs the task. What does a weak performer do? Which mistakes do you observe most often?

Helps to extract evaluation criteria and error indicators.

Before and after

Show the expert the current situation and the desired one. Ask them to outline the path of change: what needs to happen in between?

Use your CNA findings to describe the “before”.

Playing the newcomer

Ask the expert to remember being a beginner or to imagine mentoring a trainee. How would you teach them? What three things would you start with? What would you teach in the first week?

Directly counteracts the curse of knowledge.

Group output

Task 4 – Write your interview guide

Prepare the guide for the interview with your real expert. One person in the team will lead the interview, one will take notes.

  1. Write 10–14 questions covering all seven parts of the interview.
  2. Use at least four of the seven question types from the question bank.
  3. Include at least two of the four techniques: real case, employee with an error, before and after, playing the newcomer.
  4. For every question, state what you want to learn and how it will help the course.
  5. Plan the interview for 40–45 minutes. Mark the questions you can skip if time runs short.
  6. Do not ask for information you already have from the CNA.

In class task

Task 5 – Translate your jargon

The expert does not speak the language of instructional design. A good translation is plain, accurate and useful: the expert understands it, it does not change the meaning, and it helps the expert see what you need from them.

Step 1 – Compare translations

For each term, three explanations are offered. All of them sound reasonable. Discuss in your team which one you would use with your expert and why the other two are weaker. Then check.

Step 2 – Translate your own terms

List the professional terms you are likely to use with your real expert and write your plain-language versions. Think about who your expert is: a term that an examiner uses every day may mean nothing to an engineer.

Conduct the Interview

Interview the real expert using your guide, then confirm everything in writing.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this stage.
Theory

After the meeting, write it down

A verbal agreement is the most common source of later conflict. Within 24 hours of every meeting with the expert, send a short written summary and an action plan. The expert confirms or corrects it. This summary is also the basis of the Meeting Minutes in your portfolio.

A good follow-up message contains

  • the date, participants and purpose of the meeting;
  • three to five points you agreed on;
  • questions that remain open;
  • actions, with the person responsible and a deadline;
  • a request to confirm or correct by a specific date.

Turn Expertise into Learning Content

Reformulate what the expert says as observable learner actions, so that it can later become the basis of your course map.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this stage.
Theory

Seven questions for preparing learning material

  1. What should a person be able to do after the training? For example, write a formal letter that meets C1 exam requirements, or deliver a coherent academic presentation.
  2. What should they know or understand to do this? Grammar relevant to the task, common exam mistakes, academic vocabulary and collocations, model answers or examiner comments.
  3. What steps does the learner take? Read and underline key words in the task, brainstorm and structure ideas, write a first draft, review for coherence, register and accuracy, compare with a model answer or rubric.
  4. What are the typical mistakes? Neglecting paragraph structure or linking phrases, misinterpreting task requirements.
  5. How do we distinguish a good result from a poor one? Clear argument structure and appropriate register, few errors that do not impede meaning, effective academic vocabulary and cohesive devices.
  6. Do you have examples or cases?
  7. What materials can be used?
Start from actions. This logic is close to action mapping (Moore, 2017): first decide what learners must do, then add only the knowledge that supports those actions. Everything else is reference material.
In class task

Task 7 – Reformulate expert statements as learner actions

Experts usually formulate goals as knowledge: “they must know the model”. Knowing the model is not a goal. Applying the model in practice is a goal. Study the two examples, then reformulate each statement in the table below as an observable action of the learner, in the language of the design team.

Expert says: Students should know how to write an abstract.
For the design team: Students write a 150-word abstract of their own study that a journal editor accepts without structural changes.
Expert says: Managers should understand how a presentation is structured.
For the design team: Managers deliver a five-minute project update in English that colleagues can summarise in one sentence afterwards.

Handle Difficult Situations

Respond professionally when collaboration goes wrong, and organise the expert review so that feedback is useful.

Includes your group profile and all answers in this stage.
Theory

Five typical situations and how to respond

The expert does not respond or does not want to collaborate

The expert might truly be busy or might not understand what is expected.

  • Clarify the specific goal and scope of participation.
  • Emphasise the value of the expert’s input.
  • Schedule short meetings with clear outcomes.
  • Use administrative leverage if necessary, through the client contact or the person accountable in your RACI matrix.
Tip. Structure your request clearly from the start, not when you already need everything delivered.
The expert gives too much information
  • Work with content frameworks.
  • Use templates for gathering materials, such as the content matrix.
  • Guide the expert through focused questions.
The expert refuses to adapt or shorten material

The expert does not distinguish between documented knowledge and teaching content.

  • Reassure them: “We are not losing meaning, we are keeping what is applicable.”
  • If needed, move excess detail into supplementary materials.
Tip. Offer a compromise. “Let’s keep the essentials in the main course, and the rest can go into reference materials.” Ask “What should we include?” and “What can go into additional resources?”
The expert does not understand the learning goal

They may say “I’ll just explain everything, and it will make sense.”

  • Start with the key question “What should the learner be able to do after the training?”
  • Co-create a content matrix.
Example. “Knowing the model” is not a goal. “Applying the model in practice” is a goal.
The expert criticises everything and wants to rewrite it personally

Often the expert is not being difficult but simply does not understand how editing and review work.

  • Explain the revision and approval process.
  • Review step by step and document feedback.
  • Instead of defending, involve the expert: “Let’s look together. How does this solve the learning goal?”
  • Show the design logic through the chain goal – format – result.
Tip. Use a feedback template with specific questions the expert must answer. Not “What do you think?” but “Is this consistent with your professional view?”, “Is anything missing?”, “Are the formulations accurate?”
